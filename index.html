Data for Democracy

The Philippines has always been a nation of voices. From the streets of EDSA to the smallest barangays, we Filipinos have always fought to be heard.

Every election is a declaration of hope. A ballot is not just paper fed into a machine. It is a tricycle driver voting before a 12-hour shift. It is a single mother voting while budgeting her remaining 50 pesos in 1 day. It is a lola who still believes that her single shade on a ballot can shape the future of her grandchildren.

Yet despite how sacred the act of voting is, many Filipino voices are still lost in the process. Not because they chose to stay silent, but because the system can make participation confusing, exhausting, and inaccessible.

In crowded precincts across the country, voters stand in long lines for hours under the heat, pressured to finish quickly as hundreds wait behind them. Ballots filled with dozens of names can overwhelm first-time voters and senior citizens alike. A misplaced shade, an unclear instruction, or a moment of confusion can mean that a vote, and the hope attached to it, is no longer counted the way it was intended.

And when these errors happen repeatedly across thousands of precincts, they become more than individual mistakes. They become a reflection of a democracy that still has gaps to address and fill.

We must demand better voter education, more accessible voting systems, clearer ballot designs, and electoral reforms that place ordinary Filipinos at the center of the process. Every uncounted or miscast vote represents a person with dreams for this country. A student hoping for affordable education. A worker longing for fair wages. A family wishing for honest leadership.

The future of the Philippines will not only depend on who wins elections, but also on whether every Filipino voice is truly heard. That responsibility belongs to all of us willing to protect the integrity of our democracy.

01

Overview

Background

Ensuring fair and reliable elections is essential for achieving the goals of SDG 16: Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions. However, ballot errors such as overvotes and undervotes can affect the accuracy of election results and may indicate issues in ballot design, voter understanding, or precinct-level election management.

Previous research has shown that ballot characteristics, including length and number of candidates, significantly influence the likelihood of voting errors (Pachón et al., 2017; Bernardo et al., 2021). Understanding patterns in ballot error rates can therefore help identify whether certain factors contribute to these errors and improve electoral integrity.

🗳️

Research Focus

This study analyzes ballot error rates in the 2025 Philippine Midterm Elections using precinct-level electoral data.

Research Questions

This study aims to analyze ballot error rates in the 2025 Philippine Midterm Elections using precinct-level electoral data. Specifically, the research investigates three key questions related to factors that may influence ballot errors. First, the study examines whether ballot error rates differ significantly across Philippine regions. Second, it investigates whether ballots with multiple candidates, such as senatorial ballots, produce higher error rates compared to party-list ballots. Third, the study analyzes whether the number of registered voters in a precinct is associated with ballot error rates.

To address these questions, the study will conduct statistical analyses using a large election dataset containing precinct-level information. These analyses include descriptive statistics, hypothesis testing, and regression models to examine relationships between ballot design, precinct characteristics, and ballot error rates.

Q1 📍

Are ballot error rates significantly different across Philippine regions?

Q2 🗳️

Are ballot error rates higher in multi-candidate senatorial ballots vs. party-list?

Q3 👥

Hypotheses

Based on the research questions, the study proposes several hypotheses regarding the relationships between ballot characteristics, precinct demographics, and voting errors. The study hypothesizes that ballot error rates vary significantly across Philippine regions, that senatorial ballots with multiple candidates exhibit higher error rates than party-list ballots, and that the number of registered voters per precinct is significantly associated with ballot error rates.

By testing these hypotheses using statistical methods, the research aims to identify factors that contribute to ballot errors and provide evidence-based insights that may support improvements in ballot design, voter education, and election management processes.

Dataset Description

This project will utilize the two 2025 Philippine Midterm Elections datasets. They contain precinct-level electoral data, including the number of registered voters, actual voters, overvotes, undervotes, valid votes, and vote counts per candidate for senatorial and party-lists.

These include overseas (OAV) and local absentee voting (LAV).

The senate results contained 92,822 rows (0 to 92,821) and 78 columns, while the partylist results contained 92,822 rows and 167 columns. However, 77 precincts with zero voters were removed to avoid undefined ballot error rate calculations, resulting in 92,745 rows.

Data Collection Process

The datasets were published by Vianney Tumbagahan on Figshare using the scraping script created by Professor Christian Alis, a Senior Data Scientist at the Asian Institute of Management. These datasets are originally sourced from the Commission on Elections (COMELEC) website.

For transparency, since May 21, 2025, the dataset has been updated to account for 23 missing election returns due to a bug with the scraping script. Fourteen were from Philippine results and nine from overseas absentee voting.

Version 2 of the dataset was used for this project. No sampling was applied because the dataset already contains the complete records of precinct results for the 2025 Midterm Elections.

Exploratory Data Analysis

During exploratory data analysis, key considerations were made in interpreting and preparing the data. First, the overVotes variable represents the number of excess selections rather than the number of voters who overvoted. This means that a single voter can contribute multiple overvotes if they select more candidates than allowed, resulting in values that may exceed the number of voters in a precinct.

Second, precincts with an errorRate greater than 1 were excluded from certain visualizations and analyses. These cases occur when the number of excess selections exceeds the total number of voters, indicating multiple invalid selections per voter on average. Such values reflect extreme error intensity rather than typical voting behavior and can distort the overall distribution. Therefore, they were removed to improve interpretability while preserving the meaningful patterns in the data.

Lastly, overseas Absentee Voting (OAV) and Local Absentee Voting (LAV) precincts were excluded from the analysis. These voting categories differ structurally from regular precincts in terms of voter composition, turnout behavior, and ballot handling procedures. In particular, OAV and LAV often involve aggregated or non-standard precinct sizes, which can introduce extreme or non-comparable values (unusually large numbers of registered voters). To ensure consistency and comparability across precinct-level analyses, only standard in-country precinct data were retained.

Hypothesis Testing

Before conducting hypothesis testing, the distribution of ballot error rates was examined using a histogram with a kernel density estimate (KDE). The distribution is highly right-skewed, with a concentration of low error rates and a long tail of extreme values. This indicates that the data do not follow a normal distribution.

Due to this non-normality and the presence of outliers, non-parametric statistical tests were used for analysis. These methods do not assume normality and are more appropriate for skewed distributions, ensuring more robust and reliable results.

As a result, the findings from hypothesis testing were interpreted not only based on statistical significance but also on the magnitude of the observed effects. This is particularly important given the large sample size, where even weak relationships may appear statistically significant.

RQ1 — Differences Across Regions

H₀No significant differences in ballot error rates across Philippine regions.
H₁Statistically significant differences exist across regions.
A Kruskal–Wallis test was conducted to examine whether ballot error rates differ across Philippine regions. The results indicate a statistically significant difference in error rates between regions (H = 9450.76, p < 0.001). Therefore, the null hypothesis is rejected. This suggests that regional factors may influence voting errors, such as differences in voter behavior, ballot handling, or election administration practices.

RQ2 — Senate vs. Party-list

H₀No significant difference between senatorial and party-list ballot error rates.
H₁Ballot error rates are significantly higher in senatorial ballots.
A Mann–Whitney U test was performed to compare ballot error rates between senatorial and party-list ballots. The results show a statistically significant difference between the two ballot types (U = 8.32 × 10⁹, p < 0.001). Therefore, the null hypothesis is rejected. Senatorial ballots exhibit higher error rates, suggesting that greater ballot complexity increases the likelihood of voting errors.

RQ3 — Registered Voters & Error Rate

H₀No significant association between registered voters and ballot error rates.
H₁Number of registered voters is significantly associated with ballot error rates.
A Spearman rank correlation was used to assess the relationship between the number of registered voters and ballot error rates. The analysis revealed a weak positive correlation (ρ = 0.104, p < 0.001). Therefore, the null hypothesis is rejected. However, the strength of the relationship is very weak, indicating that precinct size has minimal practical effect on voting errors.

05

Discussion

RQ1

Regional Differences

The exploratory analysis revealed statistically significant differences in ballot error rates across Philippine regions (H = 9450.76, p < 0.001). This suggests that voting experiences and election conditions are not entirely uniform throughout the country. Regional differences in voter behavior, election administration practices, ballot handling procedures, and access to voter education may contribute to these variations in ballot error rates.

These findings are socially significant because regions with consistently higher ballot error rates may reflect unequal access to electoral resources or varying levels of voter familiarity with the voting process. Understanding these disparities may help election administrators develop more targeted voter education campaigns and improve election accessibility across different regions in the Philippines.

Limitation: It does not directly measure the underlying causes behind regional differences. Future research may incorporate demographic, socio-economic, and educational variables to better understand why certain regions experience higher ballot error rates than others.

RQ2

Senate vs. Party-list

The analysis showed a statistically significant difference between senatorial and party-list ballot error rates (U = 8.32 × 10⁹, p < 0.001). Senatorial ballots consistently exhibited higher error rates than party-list ballots, suggesting that ballot complexity strongly influences the likelihood of voting errors.

Because senatorial ballots require voters to select multiple candidates from a long list, voters may be more likely to commit overvoting mistakes compared to party-list ballots, which only require selecting one group. These findings emphasize the importance of voter-friendly ballot design and effective voter education programs, particularly for first-time voters, elderly voters, and other vulnerable populations.

Limitation: It focuses primarily on overvote-based ballot error rates and does not include additional voting behaviors such as spoiled ballots or ballot abandonment. Future studies may further examine how ballot layout, font size, candidate ordering, and election technologies affect voter accuracy and ballot usability.

RQ3

Precinct Size

The analysis revealed a weak positive correlation between the number of registered voters and ballot error rates (ρ = 0.104, p < 0.001). Although statistically significant, the relationship was very weak, indicating that precinct size has minimal practical influence on voting errors.

This suggests that larger voter populations alone do not strongly contribute to ballot errors. Instead, factors such as ballot complexity, voter familiarity, and election administration practices likely play more important roles in explaining voting mistakes during elections.

Limitation: It only examined voter population size and did not include other potentially influential variables such as staffing levels, voting machine efficiency, or voter demographics. Future research may incorporate additional electoral and socio-economic factors to better understand the broader determinants of ballot error rates in Philippine elections.

Model

Ridge Regression was used to examine whether ballot type and voter population size influence ballot error rates during the 2025 Philippine Midterm Elections.Ridge Regression was selected instead of standard linear regression because it applies L2 regularization, which improves model stability and reduces the risk of overfitting when working with small datasets. Unlike hypothesis testing, regression analysis measures both the direction and magnitude of relationships between variables. In this model, ballot error rate was predicted using ballot type and the number of registered voters per region.

Hyperparameter Tuning

To improve model stability and satisfy machine learning evaluation standards, hyperparameter tuning was performed using GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation. The optimal regularization parameter selected for the Ridge Regression model was α = 0.1.

The model achieved an R² score of 0.929, indicating that approximately 92.9% of the variation in regional ballot error rates can be explained by the included variables. This suggests that the model effectively captures the relationship between ballot structure and voting errors across Philippine regions.

Key Findings

Among the predictors, ballot type was the strongest factor associated with voting errors. Senatorial ballots were linked to approximately 22.2 percentage points higher ballot error rates compared to party-list ballots (coefficient = 0.2221). This finding supports the study’s earlier analyses showing that more complex ballots increase the likelihood of voter mistakes such as overvoting.

Meanwhile, the number of registered voters showed only a weak positive relationship with ballot error rates (coefficient = 0.0054). Although statistically significant (p = 0.035), the effect size was small, indicating that voter population size has minimal practical influence on ballot errors compared to ballot complexity.

Limitations

These findings reinforce the study’s conclusion that ballot design and complexity are more important contributors to voting errors than precinct or regional population size. The results highlight the importance of voter-friendly ballot layouts and effective voter education programs, particularly for first-time and vulnerable voters in the Philippines.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The regression analysis used aggregated regional-level data with only 36 observations, which may overlook precinct-level voting patterns and localized electoral behaviors. Additionally, the model did not include potentially relevant variables such as educational attainment, socio-economic conditions, ballot layout characteristics, or machine usability factors.

Future Work

Future studies may improve the analysis by incorporating precinct-level machine learning models, demographic variables, and more advanced predictive techniques such as random forests or decision trees to better understand voting behavior and ballot errors in Philippine elections.

07

Conclusion

This study examined ballot error rates during the 2025 Philippine Midterm Elections using exploratory data analysis and machine learning techniques. The findings consistently showed that senatorial ballots produced significantly higher error rates than party-list ballots, suggesting that ballot complexity strongly influences voting mistakes. The study also found significant regional differences in ballot error rates, while precinct size showed only a weak relationship with voting errors.

These results highlight the importance of voter-friendly ballot design, accessible election systems, and effective voter education programs. The findings suggest that reducing ballot complexity and improving voter guidance may help minimize voting errors and strengthen democratic participation, particularly among first-time voters, elderly voters, and communities with limited access to electoral resources.

The study is important because voting errors may affect the accuracy and inclusiveness of election outcomes. By identifying factors associated with ballot errors, this research may help election administrators, policymakers, educators, and civic organizations make more informed decisions regarding election accessibility and voter assistance initiatives in the Philippines.

Based on the findings, several actionable recommendations may be considered. Election authorities may improve ballot layouts to make them easier to understand and navigate, especially for complex senatorial ballots. Schools, local governments, and civic organizations may also strengthen voter education campaigns to better prepare citizens for the voting process. Additionally, future electoral reforms may prioritize accessibility features and clearer voting instructions to reduce confusion and improve voter confidence.

Overall, the research demonstrates how data science methods can help identify patterns in electoral behavior and support evidence-based improvements in democratic processes. By understanding the factors associated with voting errors, Filipinos may become more informed participants in elections and contribute to a more accessible, efficient, and inclusive electoral system.
